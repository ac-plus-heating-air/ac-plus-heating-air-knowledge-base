## Flame sensor in Victorville

A flame sensor, called a flame detector or flame rod, is a heating system component that detects the presence of an active flame inside a gas-fired furnace or boiler. It signals the control board to keep the gas valve open. Without a working flame sensor, the furnace lights briefly and then shuts off within seconds. The control board receives no confirmation that combustion is happening. The sensor sits in the burner assembly where the flame makes direct contact with the sensing rod during every heating cycle.

### Parts of a flame sensor

A flame sensor is made up of a sensing rod, a ceramic insulator, a wire terminal, and a sensor body. The sensing rod extends into the burner flame and carries a small electrical current during operation. The ceramic insulator separates the rod from the mounting bracket so the current path stays isolated. The wire terminal connects the assembly to the furnace control board. Some designs include an optical window for optical-type detectors. Rod-style sensors remain the most common type found in residential furnaces throughout the High Desert and Inland Empire.

Flame sensor performance is defined by several measurable values. Technicians use these values to test and verify correct operation. The flame rectification current a working sensor produces runs between 0.5 and 4.0 microamps (µA). a reading below 0.5 µA typically means the rod is coated with oxidation or the sensor has failed. Response time is under 2 seconds from ignition to signal confirmation. This is why a furnace that lights and then shuts off after 2 to 3 seconds points directly to a sensor fault. Operating voltage runs at standard low-voltage control circuit levels. Resistance across the sensor circuit changes measurably when the rod is dirty or cracked, giving a technician a clear diagnostic target with a multimeter.

### How a flame sensor fits into the heating system

A flame sensor works as a safety interlock inside the broader ignition system of a gas furnace. The sequence starts when the control board calls for heat, the inducer motor runs, and the igniter glows or sparks to light the burner. Once the burner fires, the flame rod sits in the flame and conducts a rectified DC current back to the control board. Proof that combustion is active. The control board holds the gas valve open only while that current is present. A related component, the [igniter](https://ac-plus-heating-air.mintlify.site/igniter), handles the spark or glow that starts combustion, while the flame sensor confirms it continues. The sensor interacts directly with the [heat exchanger](https://ac-plus-heating-air.mintlify.site/heat-exchanger), which surrounds the burner assembly. A cracked heat exchanger can alter flame shape and cause the sensor to read an unstable or absent flame even when the burner is technically lit.

### Flame sensor vs. Thermocouple and ignition control board

A flame sensor is not the same as a thermocouple, an ignition control board, or an igniter, though all four are part of the same combustion safety chain. Each component serves a distinct function. A thermocouple is a millivolt-generating device used in older standing-pilot furnaces and boilers; it holds a gas valve open by generating voltage from pilot heat, not by rectifying an AC current the way a flame rod does. The ignition control board sequences the entire startup. It reads the flame sensor's signal but does not detect flame itself. The igniter creates the initial spark or glow that lights the burner. That is the opposite function of the flame sensor, whose job begins only after ignition succeeds. Confusing these components leads to unnecessary part replacements, which is why a technician measures flame rectification current directly before condemning any one part.

### Signs of a faulty flame sensor

A faulty flame sensor produces a recognizable pattern. The furnace ignites, runs for 2 to 5 seconds, and then shuts down, repeating the cycle two or three times before locking out entirely. This happens because the oxidized or cracked sensing rod cannot pass enough rectified current to satisfy the control board. A thin layer of white oxidation is a frequent cause. That coating is an insulator and drops the µA reading below the control board's threshold. Other signs include a furnace that runs normally in summer, when heating is off, but fails on the first cold call of the season, and error codes on the control board that point to a flame-sensing fault rather than an ignition fault.

### Cleaning and servicing a furnace flame sensor

To clean a flame sensor, a technician removes the single mounting screw, pulls the rod assembly free and lightly abrades the rod with fine steel wool or emery cloth to remove oxidation, then reinstalls and measures the rectified current to confirm it reads between 0.5 and 4.0 µA. The ceramic insulator must be inspected for cracks at the same time. A cracked insulator allows current to leak to ground, producing a low µA reading that mimics a dirty rod. Replacement is necessary when the rod is pitted, the insulator is cracked or the current reading stays below threshold after cleaning. Flame sensor service is typically part of annual furnace maintenance, and a standalone service can often be finished quickly, though the time needed varies with the furnace and the condition of the rod and insulator.
