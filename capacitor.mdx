## Capacitor in Victorville

A capacitor, called a cap or electrical condenser, is an electrical component that stores and releases energy to start and run the motors inside heating, ventilation and air conditioning equipment, including compressors, condenser fan motors and blower motors. Capacitors are a type of electrical component, sitting in the same broad family as resistors and inductors, but where a resistor dissipates energy as heat and an inductor stores energy in a magnetic field, a capacitor stores energy in an electric field between two conductive plates separated by a dielectric material. Without a functioning cap, the motors in an HVAC system cannot generate the torque needed to start or maintain operation.

### Parts of an HVAC capacitor

A capacitor consists of four core parts: two conductive plates, a dielectric layer between them, metal terminals for electrical connection, and external leads that connect to the motor circuit. The plates are typically aluminum foil. They are wound into a cylinder to pack a large surface area into a small housing. The dielectric is the insulating material between the plates that makes energy storage possible. Terminals are the points where the capacitor connects to the motor wiring, and damaged or corroded terminals are a common cause of intermittent electrical faults in HVAC equipment.

HVAC systems use two main types: a run capacitor, which stays energized the entire time the motor runs, and a start capacitor, which delivers a short burst of energy to get the motor spinning and then drops out of the circuit. A dual-run capacitor combines both functions in one cylindrical housing, serving the compressor and the condenser fan motor from a single unit.

### Measurable attributes: capacitance, voltage rating and temperature range

Capacitors carry three key measurements printed on their label. Capacitance is measured in microfarads (µF) and describes how much energy the component can store. HVAC run capacitors typically fall between 2.5 µF and 80 µF depending on the motor they serve, and a reading outside the rated tolerance of plus or minus 6 percent signals a failing unit. Voltage rating, expressed in volts (V), is the maximum continuous voltage the capacitor can handle safely. Common HVAC ratings are 370 V and 440 V, and a replacement must meet or exceed the original voltage rating. Temperature range, measured in degrees Celsius, defines the operating limits of the dielectric material. Most HVAC-grade capacitors are rated from negative 40 °C to positive 70 °C, and sustained heat above that ceiling accelerates dielectric breakdown and shortens service life.

### How a capacitor fits into the HVAC system

A capacitor sits inside the electrical compartment of the outdoor condensing unit or air handler as part of the broader HVAC system. It enables motor operation by supplying the phase-shifted current that single-phase AC motors need to create a rotating magnetic field. A central air conditioning system typically contains at least two capacitors: one for the compressor and one for the condenser fan motor. A ductless mini split system uses capacitors in both the indoor air handler and the outdoor compressor unit. A heat pump relies on capacitors year-round because the compressor and fan motors run in both heating and cooling modes, making capacitor condition especially important before each season.

Unlike a resistor, which limits current flow, or an inductor, which resists changes in current, a capacitor passes alternating current while blocking direct current. That property is what makes it useful for phase-shifting in motor circuits rather than for simple current control. ### Bad capacitor symptoms and HVAC capacitor failure

Bad capacitor symptoms follow a recognizable pattern. The most common sign is a system that hums but does not start, because the motor is receiving voltage but lacks the stored energy needed to turn over. A condenser fan that spins slowly or only starts when given a manual push points directly to a weak run capacitor. Other signs include a system that short-cycles, trips the breaker repeatedly or produces warm air despite the compressor appearing to run. Visible signs of HVAC capacitor failure include a bulged or domed surface on the cylindrical housing, oil residue around the terminals and a burned smell near the electrical compartment.

Capacitor testing is the definitive way to confirm a failure. A technician uses a multimeter set to capacitance mode and measures the actual µF reading against the value printed on the label. A reading more than 6 percent below the rated capacitance means the component should be replaced before the motor sustains damage from repeated hard starts.
