Airflow Testing, called Ductwork Airflow Assessment, is a diagnostic process that measures air movement volume and velocity through an HVAC system. It quantifies how much conditioned air reaches each room, how much pressure the duct network generates, and how evenly temperature distributes across zones. The process confirms whether installed equipment delivers what the design specifies. Airflow Testing is a type of HVAC System Commissioning, placing it within the broader set of verification steps that confirm a system performs correctly after installation or service. Without it, performance is assumed, not measured.

What airflow testing measures

Airflow Testing measures six core physical quantities that together describe how well a duct system moves conditioned air. Air volume delivery is expressed in cubic feet per minute (CFM) and tells a technician how much air a supply register or the system as a whole is actually pushing. Air velocity is measured in feet per minute (FPM) using an anemometer traversed across the duct cross-section. Static pressure differential, recorded in inches of water column (in. W.c.), shows the resistance the blower is working against. Temperature variance, tracked in degrees Celsius across supply and return points, reveals whether heat transfer is happening at the rate the equipment rating predicts. Air pressure in Pascals (Pa) at individual registers confirms whether zones are balanced. Together these readings form the data set that an HVAC Airflow Analysis turns into a pass or fail verdict for the installation.

A duct traverse sampling collects velocity readings at multiple points across the duct cross-section rather than at a single center point. Airflow is rarely uniform wall to wall. Static pressure measurement uses a pressure gauge tapped into the supply and return plenums. These two instruments. The anemometer and the pressure gauge. Are the minimum toolkit for a complete air balancing test.

How airflow testing relates to the broader HVAC system

Airflow Testing is performed on ductwork and the air distribution system during AC installation quality verification, which places it at the intersection of equipment sizing, duct design and occupant comfort. A central air conditioning system depends on balanced CFM delivery to every branch of the duct network; a single undersized trunk or a damper set too far closed creates hot and cold spots that no thermostat adjustment can fix. The test result directly determines HVAC system performance because a blower moving less air than the coil requires will freeze the evaporator, while one moving too much air will fail to dehumidify properly.

A heat pump is especially sensitive to airflow volume because it operates in both heating and cooling modes, and the minimum CFM threshold differs between the two. A ductless mini split system bypasses the duct network entirely, so ductwork airflow testing does not apply to it, but the same velocity and static pressure principles govern the indoor air handler's fan performance. An air conditioning contractor who commissions a new system runs the air balancing test as the final confirmation that equipment, ductwork and load calculations all agree.

Common problems airflow testing identifies

Airflow restriction is the most frequent finding. It occurs when flex duct is kinked, a damper is partially closed, or a filter is undersized for the return grille. Duct leakage is the second most common problem, where supply air escapes into unconditioned attic or crawl space before reaching the living area, reducing delivered CFM without reducing blower energy draw. Both conditions appear clearly in the static pressure reading: a higher-than-specified pressure differential points to restriction, while lower-than-expected supply CFM alongside normal static pressure points to leakage upstream of the measurement point.

Duct blocking from debris, a collapsed liner, or a closed fire damper produces a sharp pressure spike on one branch while adjacent branches read normal. One finding tells the story quickly. Temperature variance across zones that exceeds the design split by more than a few degrees Celsius indicates that a branch is receiving too little air volume to condition the space it serves. These findings are exactly what ductwork airflow testing is designed to surface before a homeowner notices uneven comfort.

Where airflow testing applies

Airflow Testing applies at three distinct points in a system's life: at initial commissioning after a new installation, after any duct modification or addition of a zone, and as part of a diagnostic visit when a homeowner reports uneven temperatures or high energy use. HVAC airflow testing at commissioning establishes a baseline CFM and static pressure reading that future service visits can compare against. Numbers tell the story directly. A system that tested at 1,200 CFM at installation and now reads 900 CFM has lost roughly 25 percent of its delivery capacity, a measurable decline that points directly to a developing restriction or leak.

Airflow testing is used after a home addition or when a room is converted from unconditioned to conditioned space. The stakes are straightforward. Adding square footage to the load without re-testing the duct system leaves duct performance unverified, and the result can be a blower that is unable to meet the new demand.
