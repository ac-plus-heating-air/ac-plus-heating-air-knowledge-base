A pressure switch, called a boiler pressure switch or combustion air pressure switch, is a safety control device that opens or closes electrical contacts based on changes in gas or air pressure inside a heating or cooling system, protecting the heat exchanger, inducer motor, burner assembly, draft inducer and connected electrical circuits from operating under unsafe pressure conditions. Component parts of a pressure switch

A pressure switch is built from five core parts: a diaphragm, a microswitch, a calibration spring, electrical contacts, and the spring housing that holds the assembly together. The diaphragm flexes inward or outward as pressure changes, translating physical force into mechanical movement. That movement trips the microswitch. The microswitch then opens or closes the electrical circuit. The calibration spring sets the exact activation threshold, and the electrical contacts carry the low-voltage signal to the control board. Each part works in sequence. A failure in any one of them stops the entire safety chain.

Measurable attributes and rated values

A pressure switch carries several rated values that define how and when it acts. Activation pressure is the point at which the switch trips, typically between 0.5 and 5.0 inches of water column (inWC) for residential furnace and boiler applications. Differential pressure, the gap between the open and close set points, runs from 0.2 to 1.0 inWC and prevents the switch from chattering on small fluctuations. The operating pressure range for common HVAC switches spans roughly 0.1 to 100 PSI depending on the application, while the switching differential, the hysteresis band, falls between 0.1 and 1.0 PSI. Contact rating is typically 5A at 24V AC, which matches the low-voltage control circuits found in most residential furnaces and heat pumps. The electrical rating of 24V AC at 3A is the most common spec for residential equipment, though commercial units carry higher ratings. A heat pump system uses pressure switches rated to the same 24V control-circuit standard, with activation pressure tuned to the refrigerant-side or airside pressures the unit produces.

How a pressure switch fits into the heating system

A pressure switch is a safety component inside the broader category of control devices that govern furnace and boiler operation. It monitors and controls air pressure to confirm that the inducer motor is running and that combustion gases are moving safely through the flue before the gas valve opens. The control board sends 24V to the inducer motor first; once the inducer builds enough negative pressure, the diaphragm flexes and the switch closes, signaling the board to proceed with ignition. Without that closed signal, the board locks out the burner entirely. A central air conditioning system uses a related pressure-sensing device on the refrigerant circuit, though the furnace pressure switch specifically monitors combustion airflow rather than refrigerant pressure. A ductless mini split system carries its own high- and low-pressure switches on the refrigerant lines, which serve the same protective function in a different circuit.

Pressure switch versus other safety controls

A pressure switch is not the same as a high limit switch, a flame rollout switch, a thermostat, or a flow switch, even though all of them protect the heating system. The high limit switch responds to temperature, not pressure, cutting power when the heat exchanger overheats. The flame rollout switch detects combustion escaping outside the burner box. A different failure mode entirely. A thermostat measures ambient air temperature and sends a call for heat. It plays no role in verifying combustion airflow. A flow switch detects liquid movement in hydronic systems rather than gas or air pressure. The furnace pressure switch specifically confirms inducer-motor performance by measuring the negative pressure the motor creates, functioning as a safety gate the system must pass before any fuel is introduced.

Common problems and signs of a faulty pressure switch

A faulty pressure switch is one of the most frequent causes of a furnace locking out on a no-heat call. The most common symptom is a system that starts the inducer motor, runs it for 15 to 30 seconds and then shuts down without igniting. A cracked or disconnected hose between the inducer housing and the switch port lets pressure bleed off, so the diaphragm never flexes far enough to close the contacts. A stuck-open switch fails to close even when pressure is correct, while a stuck-closed switch bypasses the safety check entirely, which is the more dangerous failure. Condensate blocking the pressure port produces the same no-close symptom as a cracked hose. A technician checks the switch by measuring the actual pressure at the port with a manometer and comparing it to the rated activation pressure stamped on the switch body, then testing continuity across the contacts with a multimeter. Replacing a faulty pressure switch with one matched to the original activation pressure and contact rating restores normal lockout behavior. Where pressure switches apply across HVAC equipment

Pressure switches appear in gas furnaces, boilers, induced-draft and condensing appliances, and some heat pump air handlers. In a gas furnace the combustion air pressure switch, sometimes called a draft-proving switch, sits on the inducer housing and monitors negative pressure in the flue path. In a boiler the boiler pressure switch monitors system water pressure on the hydronic side, opening the circuit if pressure drops below a safe minimum or rises above the rated maximum. Condensing furnaces often carry two switches: one for the inducer and one for the secondary heat exchanger, each set to a different activation pressure because the two stages produce different draft levels. The activation pressure on a condensing-furnace secondary switch typically falls in the 1.0 to 2.0 inWC range, lower than the primary switch, because the secondary exchanger operates at reduced draft. Matching the replacement switch to the exact activation pressure and electrical rating on the original label is the single most important step in any pressure-switch service call.
