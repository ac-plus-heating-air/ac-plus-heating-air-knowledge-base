What ductwork installation is and what it covers

Ductwork installation is the complete process of designing and fitting the air distribution network that moves conditioned air from the air handler to every room in a building. The work begins with layout planning, where a technician maps supply and return paths, then moves through duct assembly, fitting transitions and connectors, wrapping insulation, and sealing every joint before the system is tested. A finished duct system connects the air handler to the supply registers and return grilles, forming a closed loop that the blower pressurizes on every cycle.

Ductwork installation is not the same as duct cleaning, ductwork repair, or ductwork removal, and it is not a substitute for a ductless mini-split installation or a zoned VRF system installation. Those are separate services with different scopes. New ductwork installation applies when a building has no existing duct network, when the existing one is beyond repair, or when a new HVAC system requires a layout the old ducts cannot support. In homes that rely on central forced-air systems, air conditioning installation almost always includes ductwork installation as a parallel scope of work.

Measurable attributes of a ductwork installation

Ductwork installation is defined by a set of measurable attributes that determine whether the system performs correctly after the equipment is running. These attributes are the basis for design decisions, material selection, and post-installation testing.

  • Duct diameter: Duct diameter runs from 4 inches on small branch runs to 20 inches on large trunk lines, and the correct size for each branch is calculated from the airflow that zone requires.
  • Static pressure rating: Static pressure rating targets a range of 0.1 to 0.5 inches of water gauge across the system; readings outside that range point to undersized ducts, excessive fittings, or a blocked return.
  • Airflow capacity: Airflow capacity is measured in CFM per zone and must match the equipment's rated output so no room is over- or under-supplied.
  • Total linear feet installed: Total linear feet installed sets the material and labor scope of the project; a typical single-story home in Victorville may require several hundred linear feet of duct between trunk lines and branch runs.
  • Duct material type: Duct material type is most commonly galvanized steel for trunk lines and flexible duct for branch connections, though rigid fiberglass duct board is used in some residential applications.
  • Air leakage rate: Air leakage rate is expressed in CFM per 100 square feet of duct surface; a well-sealed system tests below the threshold required by California's Title 24 energy code, which governs new ductwork installation and sealing in the state.

Getting these numbers right before fabrication begins is what separates a system that performs from one that wastes energy. AC Plus Heating and Air sizes each duct system to the specific layout and load of the building rather than reusing a generic template.

How ductwork installation fits into the broader HVAC system

Ductwork installation is a step within the larger scope of HVAC Installation, sitting between equipment selection and final commissioning. The duct network is the delivery mechanism for everything the air handler produces: without it, a correctly sized AC unit cannot move conditioned air to the spaces that need it. Coordinating the duct layout with equipment specifications ensures that static pressure, airflow capacity, and register placement all align with the unit's rated performance — work that an air conditioning contractor carries out as part of system design.

The relationship between duct geometry and equipment output is direct. A duct system with too much resistance forces the blower to work harder, raises static pressure above 0.5 inches of water gauge, and shortens the life of the air handler. A system with too little resistance can cause the unit to short-cycle. Duct system installation is therefore engineered alongside the equipment selection, not added as an afterthought. Signs a building needs new ductwork installation

New ductwork installation is warranted when the existing duct network shows specific, measurable failures rather than minor wear. Recognizing these signs early prevents the AC equipment from compensating for duct problems by running longer cycles and consuming more energy.

  • Uneven room temperatures: Uneven temperatures across rooms often trace to undersized branch ducts, collapsed flexible sections, or a return air plenum that cannot draw enough air back to the handler.
  • High air leakage rate: An air leakage rate above the Title 24 threshold means a significant share of conditioned air escapes into unconditioned attic or crawl space before reaching the living area.
  • Visible duct damage: Visible damage such as crushed flexible duct, disconnected fittings, or corroded galvanized steel sections indicates the network has degraded past the point where ductwork repair is cost-effective.
  • Age beyond useful life: Duct systems in Victorville homes built before the mid-1980s may use materials that have exceeded their useful life and no longer hold shape or seal reliably.
  • Static pressure outside range: A measured static pressure consistently above 0.5 inches of water gauge with a properly functioning blower points to a duct layout that cannot support the equipment connected to it.

Where air distribution system installation applies

Air distribution system installation applies to any building that uses a central forced-air system to heat or cool occupied space. In Victorville and the broader High Desert region, that covers single-family homes, multi-family buildings, light commercial spaces, and new construction projects where no duct network exists yet. New construction is the most straightforward application. The duct layout can be planned before walls are closed, allowing trunk lines to run through conditioned space rather than a hot attic. Duct material type and insulation thickness are both important factors in energy retention under those conditions.

Retrofit installations in existing homes are more complex. Technicians must route new duct sections through finished walls and ceilings, work around existing framing, and connect to an air handler that may be in a garage, closet, or attic. Total linear feet installed in a retrofit often exceeds a new-construction equivalent because indirect routing is sometimes the only option.
