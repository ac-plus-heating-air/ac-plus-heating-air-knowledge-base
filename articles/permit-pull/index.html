Permit Pull, called the Permitting Process, is a type of administrative process in which a homeowner or licensed contractor submits an application to a local government authority to obtain official authorization before beginning construction, installation, or replacement work on a heating and cooling system, including central air conditioning systems, ductless mini split systems, heat pumps and related mechanical equipment. The permit pull precedes any physical installation work and is the legal checkpoint that connects the project to local building codes, inspection schedules and municipal records.

What permit pull is and why it matters for AC installation

Permit pull is the formal authorization step that makes an AC installation legal under California building law. Without a pulled permit, the work is classified as unpermitted work, which creates liability for the property owner and can complicate home sales, insurance claims and future inspections. The permit acquisition process exists to confirm that the planned system meets local mechanical and energy codes before a single wire is touched or a refrigerant line is run.

A licensed air conditioning contractor handles permit pull on behalf of the homeowner in most residential projects, submitting the required documentation and coordinating with the local building department. Measurable attributes of the permit pull process

Permit pull has several concrete, trackable attributes that determine how a project is scheduled and budgeted.

  • Processing time: Processing time is the number of business days between application submission and permit issuance. Residential AC permit pull in San Bernardino County typically runs 5 to 15 business days for standard over-the-counter applications, though complex projects or backlogged departments can extend that window.
  • Fee: The fee is the dollar amount the jurisdiction charges to issue the permit. Mechanical permit fees in California vary by municipality and are calculated on the scope of work, equipment type and sometimes the valuation of the installation.
  • Documentation required: Documentation required includes the completed application form, equipment specifications (make, model, SEER rating), load calculation data, site plan or property description, and contractor license information. Some jurisdictions require a Manual J load calculation for new system installations.
  • Status: Status is the current state of the permit in the municipal system: pending, approved, denied or closed. A permit moves to closed status only after the final inspection passes, which confirms the installed system matches what was approved.
  • Time required: Time required covers the full span from initial application to final inspection sign-off, not just the issuance window. For a residential permit pull on a central air conditioning system, the total time from application to closed status commonly runs 2 to 6 weeks depending on inspection scheduling and any correction notices.

How permit pull relates to the broader installation system

Permit pull is the upstream administrative process that precedes every permitted AC installation. It connects directly to the equipment being installed: a ductless mini split system and a ducted central system each require their own permit application because the scope of work, refrigerant line requirements and electrical load differ between the two. A heat pump installation that involves both heating and cooling functions may require a combined mechanical and electrical permit, adding a second permit acquisition step to the project.

The permit pull determines the inspection schedule. Once the permit is approved and work begins, the local building department assigns one or more inspection checkpoints, typically a rough-in inspection before walls are closed and a final inspection after the system is operational. The project cannot legally close until the inspector signs off on both.

Residential permit pull for AC projects in Victorville

Residential permit pull in Victorville falls under the City of Victorville Building and Safety Division, which operates under California Building Code Title 24 mechanical requirements. The process has specific documentation requirements. An AC permit pull application for a standard split system replacement requires the equipment data sheet, the contractor's C-20 HVAC license number, and the property address. New construction or system upsizing may require a load calculation report to confirm the selected equipment matches the home's thermal envelope.

Permit acquisition for a straightforward equipment swap, where the new unit matches the existing system type and capacity, is generally the fastest path through the process. Changing system type, for example replacing a gas furnace and AC combination with a heat pump, typically triggers additional review because it changes the fuel source and may require electrical panel upgrades that carry their own permit.

Unpermitted work and the risks of skipping permit pull

Unpermitted work is the direct contrast to a completed permit pull. Work performed without a permit is not inspected. This means code violations, improper refrigerant handling, undersized electrical circuits, and incorrect ductwork connections go undetected until a problem surfaces. In California, unpermitted HVAC work discovered during a home sale can require the seller to retroactively permit and re-inspect the installation. A process that sometimes means opening walls to verify work that was never inspected at rough-in.

Insurance carriers may deny claims tied to unpermitted mechanical systems. Some homeowner policies include exclusions for damage caused by non-code-compliant equipment. The permit pull step adds days to the project timeline, but it is the mechanism that keeps the installation legally defensible.
