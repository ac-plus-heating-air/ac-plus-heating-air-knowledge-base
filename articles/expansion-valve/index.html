An expansion valve, called a TXV (thermostatic expansion valve), is a refrigerant metering device that reduces high-pressure liquid refrigerant to a low-pressure state and controls exactly how much refrigerant enters the evaporator coil, making it one of the four core components in every refrigeration cycle alongside the compressor, condenser and evaporator. As a type of refrigerant metering device, the TXV sits between the condenser outlet and the evaporator inlet, where it performs the pressure drop that allows the refrigerant to absorb heat from indoor air. Without precise metering at this point, the entire central air conditioning system loses the ability to regulate cooling output.

What an expansion valve is and how it works

An expansion valve is a refrigerant metering device. It converts high-pressure liquid refrigerant into a low-pressure, low-temperature mixture by forcing it through a calibrated orifice. The TXV does this continuously, adjusting flow in real time based on the superheat reading at the evaporator outlet. Its five main component parts are the valve body, sensing bulb, orifice seat, diaphragm, and orifice. The sensing bulb mounts to the suction line and sends a pressure signal to the diaphragm, which opens or closes the orifice seat to increase or decrease refrigerant flow. A fixed orifice device meters refrigerant at a single unchanging opening and cannot respond to load changes the way a TXV does.

Measurable attributes of an expansion valve

Expansion valve performance is defined by several specific, measurable attributes that technicians verify during installation and service. Operating pressure across a TXV typically ranges from 100 to 600 psi, depending on the refrigerant type and system design. Superheat is the temperature of the refrigerant vapor above its saturation point at the evaporator outlet. It is factory-set or field-adjusted to a target of 5 to 15°F. Capacity is rated in BTU/hr and must match the system's tonnage so the valve neither starves nor floods the evaporator coil. Refrigerant type determines valve selection. A valve sized for R-410A carries different internal geometry than one sized for R-22 or R-32, and substituting the wrong valve degrades both capacity and superheat control. Refrigerant work on any of these systems requires EPA 608-certified handling by a licensed technician. How the expansion valve relates to the refrigeration cycle

The expansion valve regulates refrigerant flow directly into the evaporator coil, which is the heat-absorbing side of the refrigeration cycle. High-pressure liquid leaves the condenser, passes through the TXV, and arrives at the evaporator as a cold, low-pressure mixture ready to absorb heat. The compressor sits on the opposite end of the cycle, pulling low-pressure vapor out of the evaporator and compressing it back to high pressure. The compressor and the expansion valve are functional opposites, one building pressure and one releasing it. In a heat pump, the TEV must meter refrigerant in both directions because the cycle reverses between heating and cooling modes, which is why heat pump expansion valves are often bidirectional or paired with a check valve assembly. A ductless mini split system uses the same TXV principle but locates the valve inside the indoor air handler, closer to the evaporator coil it serves.

Faulty expansion valve symptoms and common problems

Faulty expansion valve symptoms fall into two categories: an over-feeding valve and an under-feeding valve. An over-feeding TXV floods the evaporator with too much refrigerant, causing frost on the suction line, liquid slugging back to the compressor and low superheat readings below 5°F. An under-feeding valve starves the evaporator, producing high superheat above 15°F, warm supply air and a system that short-cycles because it cannot meet the thermostat setpoint. Other common problems include a stuck sensing bulb that has lost its charge, a clogged orifice seat from debris in the refrigerant circuit, and a diaphragm that has lost flexibility after years of pressure cycling. A technician diagnoses TXV faults by measuring suction pressure, discharge pressure and superheat simultaneously rather than replacing the valve on symptom alone. A licensed air conditioning contractor should perform any expansion valve replacement to protect the compressor and preserve the manufacturer's warranty coverage for the homeowner. Where expansion valve replacement applies

Expansion valve replacement applies to any split-system or packaged unit that uses a TXV rather than a fixed orifice as its metering device. Commercial systems commonly use a TXV for variable load control. Many higher-capacity residential systems do as well. A fixed orifice is simpler and has no moving parts, but it is calibrated for one specific operating condition and loses efficiency as outdoor temperature or indoor load shifts. Replacement is required any time a system is converted to a different refrigerant type, because the valve orifice sizing and sensing bulb charge must match the new refrigerant's pressure-temperature curve. In Victorville's climate, summer ambient temperatures regularly push condensing pressures toward the upper end of the 100 to 600 psi operating range. A correctly sized and calibrated TXV is the difference between a system that holds setpoint and one that runs continuously without satisfying the thermostat.
