Igniter in Victorville

An igniter, called a hot surface igniter or spark igniter, is a heating system component. It generates the heat or electrical spark needed to ignite the gas burner inside a furnace or other combustion-based heating unit. Igniters sit at the start of every heating cycle. Without a working igniter, the burner never lights and the system produces no heat. The igniter connects directly to the furnace control board, which sends voltage to the component on a call for heat, triggering combustion within seconds.

Parts and construction of an igniter

An igniter is built from a small set of components, each with a specific role in producing and sustaining the spark or surface heat that starts combustion. The heating element is the core part. It is the section that glows or arcs to reach ignition temperature. Two electrodes carry the electrical charge to the element, while a ceramic base and ceramic insulator hold the assembly in place and prevent the high-voltage current from grounding out on the furnace cabinet. Electrical terminals at the end of the assembly connect the igniter to the furnace wiring harness.

  • Heating element: Heating element is the active section that reaches ignition temperature, typically made from silicon nitride or silicon carbide.
  • Electrodes: Electrodes carry the electrical charge from the control board to the heating element.
  • Ceramic base: Ceramic base anchors the assembly inside the burner compartment and withstands extreme heat without deforming.
  • Ceramic insulator: Ceramic insulator prevents current from arcing to the furnace cabinet or surrounding metal.
  • Electrical terminals: Electrical terminals are the connection points that link the igniter to the furnace wiring harness.

Measurable attributes: voltage, resistance, current draw and lifespan

Igniters carry specific electrical ratings that determine compatibility with a given furnace model. Most residential hot surface igniters operate at 120 volts. Some older or commercial units use 24-volt or 240-volt designs. Resistance across the heating element typically falls between 40 and 90 ohms when the igniter is cold. A reading outside that range is a reliable sign the component is failing. Current draw during the warm-up phase runs between 3 and 4 amps for a standard 120-volt silicon carbide igniter, dropping as the element reaches operating temperature.

Lifespan is the attribute homeowners notice most. A silicon carbide igniter lasts roughly 3 to 5 years under normal use, while a silicon nitride igniter can last 7 to 10 years because the material is less brittle and more resistant to thermal shock. The ignition system as a whole depends on the igniter staying within its rated resistance window. A cracked or degraded element raises resistance, reduces current flow and produces a weak or absent flame.

How the igniter fits into the heating system

Igniters are used in furnaces and boilers, making them one of the most common components a heating technician replaces. The igniter sits inside the burner compartment. It is positioned directly above or beside the gas burner ports. When the thermostat calls for heat, the control board energizes the igniter for 15 to 90 seconds, long enough for the element to reach 1,800 to 2,500 degrees Fahrenheit, then opens the gas valve so the burner lights on contact. Furnace models determine exact timing and placement.

The heat exchanger is the component immediately downstream: combustion gases from the burner pass through the heat exchanger, transferring warmth to the air supply before the gases exit through the flue. A failed igniter means no combustion, so the heat exchanger never warms and the blower circulates unheated air. Furnace igniter replacement is therefore one of the first diagnostics a technician performs when a furnace runs but produces no heat. Emergency heater repair often begins with this exact sequence.

Common igniter problems and signs of failure

Igniter malfunction shows up in a predictable set of symptoms that distinguish it from other furnace faults. The most direct sign is a furnace that cycles. The blower starts, the system runs through a call for heat, but no warm air arrives because the burner never lit. No warm air arrives. A cracked heating element is a common physical cause, often visible as a hairline fracture across the glowing surface.

A flame sensor is a related but separate component. The flame sensor confirms that combustion is present after the igniter fires, while the igniter itself only starts the process. A limit switch is another distinct part: it monitors heat exchanger temperature and shuts the furnace down if overheating occurs, which is the opposite function from the igniter's job of initiating heat. Confusing these three components leads to misdiagnosis, so a technician measures resistance at the igniter terminals before replacing any part.

  • No heat despite normal blower operation: No heat despite normal blower operation is the clearest sign of igniter failure, because the fan runs on a separate circuit from the igniter.
  • Cracked or broken element: Cracked or broken element is visible on inspection and causes an open circuit, confirmed by a resistance reading of infinity on a multimeter.
  • Short cycling: Short cycling occurs when the igniter glows but produces insufficient heat to light the burner, triggering a safety lockout after two or three failed attempts.
  • Resistance out of range: Resistance out of range, below 40 ohms or above 90 ohms on a cold igniter, indicates a degraded element before visible cracking appears.
  • Delayed ignition: Delayed ignition happens when the element is weakening and takes longer than the control board's timed window to reach ignition temperature, causing a gas puff on lighting.

Igniter replacement and service in Victorville

Furnace igniter replacement requires shutting off the gas supply and disconnecting the furnace from power. The repair involves handling a fragile ceramic component. Skin oils from bare hands can create hot spots that crack a new silicon carbide element before it ever fires. A technician matches the replacement igniter to the furnace model by cross-referencing the OEM part number, then verifies the repair with a measured resistance check and a full heating cycle test. Voltage, resistance range, and physical mounting configuration must all match. The replacement must match the original specifications exactly.
