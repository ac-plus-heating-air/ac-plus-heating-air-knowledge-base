Air vents, called air registers or diffusers, are openings fitted with grilles, louvers, damper blades and mounting frames that allow conditioned air to enter a room, control the direction of airflow and regulate how much air each space receives. Air vents are a type of Air Distribution System Component, sitting at the final delivery point of a central air conditioning system where treated air leaves the ductwork and enters the living space. Every room in a forced-air home relies on at least one supply vent to receive cooled or heated air, and a separate return grille to send air back to the air handler.

What air vents are and how they work

Air vents are the terminal fittings of a forced-air distribution system, connecting the duct network to the conditioned space through a grille face that faces the room. A register differs from a plain grille in one key way: it includes an adjustable damper blade that lets the occupant open, partially close or fully shut the vent. A diffuser spreads airflow in multiple directions at once, making it common on ceilings where even distribution across a large area matters. Supply vents push conditioned air into the room; return vents pull room air back toward the air handler for reconditioning. These are not the same thing, and mixing them up is a common source of comfort problems.

Air vents connect directly to ductwork and are installed by an AC technician as part of the broader air distribution system. In a ductless mini split system, there are no supply vents at all. The air handler mounts on the wall and delivers air directly, which is one reason homeowners choose that option when adding a room without existing ductwork.

Measurable attributes of air vents

Air vents carry several attributes that determine whether a room receives the right volume and direction of air. Size matters. Standard vent opening sizes run from 2 inches to 12 inches, with 4x10-inch and 6x12-inch rectangular registers being the most common residential sizes. Airflow rate is measured in cubic feet per minute, or CFM, and varies by duct pressure and vent size. A properly sized 6x12-inch register typically handles 50 to 100 CFM depending on system static pressure. The attributes that matter most when selecting or replacing a vent are listed below.

  • Vent opening size: Vent opening size ranges from 2 to 12 inches in diameter for round vents and up to 6x12 inches or larger for rectangular registers, matched to the duct collar it connects to.
  • Airflow direction: Airflow direction is set by fixed or adjustable louvers; a two-way register deflects air in two directions, while a four-way diffuser spreads it in four, making it better suited to ceiling installation.
  • Damper type: Damper type is either fixed (no adjustment) or adjustable; an adjustable damper blade lets the occupant balance airflow room by room without touching the thermostat.
  • Material composition: Material composition is steel, aluminum or plastic; steel and aluminum registers handle higher temperatures and last longer, while plastic units are lighter and resist corrosion in humid climates like the high desert.
  • Dimensions: Dimensions are stated as the face size (the visible grille) and the duct opening size (the collar); these two numbers are not the same, and ordering the wrong one means the vent will not seat flush against the wall or floor.
  • Airflow rate (CFM): Airflow rate is the volume of air the vent passes per minute; undersized vents restrict airflow and raise system static pressure, which shortens blower motor life.

Air vents blocked by furniture and other common problems

Blocked vents are a frequently reported comfort complaint. Air vents obstructed by furniture, rugs, or drapes restrict airflow in ways that affect the entire system, not only the blocked room. A blocked supply vent raises static pressure in the duct, forcing the blower to work harder and reducing airflow to every other room on the same branch. Closing too many registers at once creates the same problem. The system was designed for a specific total CFM, and restricting outlets does not reduce the load on the equipment the way homeowners expect.

Beyond blockages, the problems that show up most often with registers and diffusers are listed below. AC Plus Heating and Air serves homeowners in Victorville, CA, Wrightwood, CA, Rancho Cucamonga, CA, and Redlands, CA who encounter these issues.

  • Rattling grille face: Rattling grille face means the mounting screws have loosened or the duct collar has shifted; tightening the frame stops the noise without replacing the vent.
  • Bent or stuck louvers: Bent or stuck louvers prevent the damper from opening fully, cutting the CFM that room receives and making one area noticeably warmer or cooler than the rest of the house.
  • Dust and debris buildup: Dust and debris buildup on the grille face restricts airflow and pushes particulates back into the room every time the blower cycles on; cleaning the grille face every three to six months keeps airflow clear.
  • Condensation on the register: Condensation on the register face signals that supply air is significantly colder than the surrounding surface, often pointing to an oversized system or a duct that runs through an unconditioned space.
  • Disconnected duct at the collar: Disconnected duct at the collar means conditioned air is dumping into the wall cavity or attic instead of the room, a loss that drives up runtime and energy use.

How air vents relate to the broader AC system

Air vents are part of the air conditioning system as a whole, sitting downstream of the air handler, the evaporator coil and the ductwork. The air conditioning contractor sizes each supply vent to match the duct branch feeding it, so the total CFM delivered across all registers equals what the air handler moves. A heat pump uses the same duct and register network as a conventional split system, meaning vent sizing and placement decisions made during installation affect heating performance in winter as much as cooling in summer. More on how heat pumps use the same distribution path is at the heat pump reference.

Return grilles, duct caps, exhaust fans, and air intakes are related but distinct components. Return grilles are never closed. They pull air back to the air handler and carry no damper, making closure incorrect in all circumstances. Exhaust fans move air out of the building entirely, not back into the duct system. Duct caps seal unused duct openings and carry no airflow at all. Air vents, by contrast, are always supply-side fittings that deliver conditioned air into the occupied space.

Installing air vents: placement and sizing rules

Vent location determines comfort. Installing air vents in the right position determines whether a room reaches setpoint quickly or struggles to stay comfortable. Supply registers go on exterior walls or under windows in older design practice, placing conditioned air where heat gain or loss is greatest. Ceiling diffusers work well in open floor plans because the four-way spread reaches more of the room from a single outlet. Floor registers suit rooms with baseboard-level duct runs and work especially well for heating, since warm air rises naturally from the floor.

Vent placement follows the duct layout, which an AC technician sizes using load calculations for the specific room volume and exposure. Size mismatches cause real problems. A register that is too small for its duct branch creates a high-velocity jet of air that causes noise and drafts. One that is too large for the branch delivers sluggish airflow that never fully conditions the far corners of the room. Matching the vent face size to the duct collar, and the duct collar to the calculated CFM for that room, is the correct sequence.
