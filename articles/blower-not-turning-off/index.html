Blower not turning off in Victorville

Blower Not Turning Off, called Blower Stuck On, is an HVAC fault where the air handler or furnace blower motor continues running after the heating cycle ends. It keeps airflow active through the supply registers. The motor draws power continuously, circulating unconditioned air through the duct system long past the point the thermostat called for heat. Blower Not Turning Off is a type of HVAC System Malfunction, sitting in the same fault category as failed ignition sequences, refrigerant loss, and heat exchanger cracks. Components involved in a blower not turning off

Blower Not Turning Off traces to three components: the blower relay, the control board, and the blower motor. The relay is the most common single point of failure. A relay welded shut in the closed position sends continuous voltage to the motor regardless of what the thermostat signals. The control board is the second most frequent cause. A burned trace or failed output circuit keeps the fan-on signal live even after the heat call drops. The motor itself rarely causes continuous run on its own. However, a motor with a shorted winding can draw current in a way that mimics a stuck relay. Troubleshooting means isolating which of these three components is holding the circuit closed. HVAC ductwork carries the continuous airflow produced by this fault.

Measurable attributes of continuous blower run

Continuous blower run has several measurable attributes that distinguish it from normal post-cycle fan operation. Runtime after the heat call ends is the clearest indicator. Under normal operation, the blower runs a short purge cycle of roughly 90 seconds to 3 minutes after the burner shuts off. A blower not turning off runs indefinitely, with continuous operation measured in hours rather than minutes. Supply air temperature drops steadily once the heat exchanger cools. Supply air reading near room temperature 10 minutes after the heat call is a reliable sign the blower is stuck on rather than completing a normal purge. Blower motor amperage during a stuck-on condition matches full-load running amperage, typically 3 to 8 amps depending on motor size, with no drop-off. Power draw stays at the motor's rated wattage continuously. A half-horsepower blower motor draws roughly 370 to 400 watts, so a blower stuck on for 24 hours adds close to 9 kilowatt-hours of consumption. Noise level stays constant at the motor's normal operating decibel range rather than ramping down, which is often the first thing a homeowner notices at night. Airflow state remains active at all registers, delivering room-temperature or slightly cool air that feels uncomfortable during cold weather.

How blower not turning off relates to the heating system

Blower Not Turning Off affects the heating system as a whole, not the blower circuit alone. The blower runs without a heat call. The heat exchanger cools faster than designed, which can cause the high-limit switch to reset incorrectly on the next cycle. The furnace fan stuck on condition depressurizes return-air pathways continuously, pulling unconditioned air through envelope gaps and raising infiltration loads. A stuck blower relay or failed control board that causes this fault is the same board or relay that governs ignition sequencing, so a system showing continuous blower run should be inspected for secondary faults before the next heating season. A qualified air conditioning contractor can test relay continuity and board output signals with a multimeter to confirm which component is holding the circuit live. Blower not turning off versus related HVAC faults

Blower Not Turning Off is the opposite of Blower Not Starting, where the motor fails to run at all after a heat call. Normal Blower Operation sits between these two extremes: the motor starts within 30 to 60 seconds of a heat call, runs through the cycle and stops within 3 minutes of burner shutoff. Short cycling is a related but distinct fault where the system starts and stops too frequently rather than running continuously. A system that short cycles may eventually develop a stuck-on blower if the underlying cause stresses the control board. Blower Not Turning On is the direct antonym: no airflow state, no power draw at the motor, and a heat exchanger that overheats and trips the high-limit switch. Understanding where Blower Not Turning Off sits among these faults helps a technician narrow the diagnosis before pulling components. Furnace service often reveals this exact sequence of faults.

Why is my blower not turning off. Common causes by frequency

Why is my blower not turning off is one of the most searched heating questions in cold-weather months, and the answer almost always points to one of four causes. The causes below are ordered from most to least common based on field patterns.

  • Stuck blower relay: Blower relay contacts weld shut under repeated switching load, keeping the motor circuit closed permanently. Replacing the relay restores normal operation.
  • Failed control board: Control board output traces burn or short, holding the fan-on signal active. A board failure often shows secondary symptoms like erratic ignition or fault codes.
  • Thermostat set to fan-on: Thermostat fan setting left on "ON" rather than "AUTO" runs the blower continuously by design. This is not a fault but is frequently misread as one. Switching the fan setting to "AUTO" stops the blower between heat calls.
  • Wiring fault: A shorted wire between the control board and the blower relay can mimic a stuck relay. Wiring faults are less common but harder to locate without a wiring diagram and a continuity tester.

Blower not turning off after heat. Where this fault applies

Blower Not Turning Off after heat is most common in gas furnaces with single-stage or two-stage burners, where a dedicated blower relay handles the fan circuit separately from the gas valve circuit. The fault appears in air handlers paired with heat pumps. In that configuration, the control board manages both the reversing valve and the blower motor, and a board failure can lock the blower on while the compressor cycles normally. Older systems with electromechanical relays are more prone to contact welding than modern variable-speed air handlers, which use integrated motor controllers that fail differently. In light commercial HVAC applications, a furnace fan stuck on condition in a rooftop unit can go unnoticed for days. The equipment is out of sight. That delay makes the power consumption impact larger before the fault is caught.
