HVAC ductwork, called ducting, is a network of air distribution channels that carries conditioned air from a heating or cooling unit to every room in a building, including supply ducts that push treated air out, return ducts that pull stale air back, plenums, fittings, and vents. As a core component of the central air conditioning system, ductwork is classified as part of the broader HVAC Air Distribution System. Without a properly sized and sealed duct network, even a high-efficiency air conditioner or furnace cannot deliver consistent comfort throughout a home.

What HVAC ductwork is and how it works

HVAC ductwork is the physical pathway that moves conditioned air from the air handler to each room and back again. Air ducts operate in two directions: supply ducts carry cooled or heated air out through registers, and return ducts pull room air back to the unit for reconditioning. The system connects directly to the air conditioning contractor-installed equipment at the air handler or furnace, making the duct network the delivery mechanism for every BTU the equipment produces.

Ductwork materials fall into three main categories: galvanized steel, aluminum, and flexible duct. Galvanized steel is the most common choice for main trunk lines because of its rigidity and long service life. Flexible duct, a wire-coil core wrapped in plastic and insulation, handles branch runs to individual registers where rigid sections are impractical.

Measurable attributes of HVAC ductwork

HVAC ductwork sizing is determined by several physical and performance attributes that govern how well air moves through the system. Getting these numbers right during HVAC ductwork installation is essential to a system that cools every room evenly and avoids hot spots at the far end of the house.

  • Duct diameter: Diameter ranges from 4 inches for small branch runs to 24 inches for large trunk lines, and the correct size depends on the airflow volume each branch must carry.
  • Airflow capacity: Airflow is measured in cubic feet per minute (CFM); a single bedroom register typically needs 50 to 150 CFM, while a whole-home system can move 2,000 CFM or more through the main trunk.
  • Leakage rate: Leakage rate is the percentage of total airflow that escapes through gaps, loose joints, or unsealed connections; a well-sealed system keeps leakage below 5 percent of total airflow, while a leaky system can lose 20 to 30 percent.
  • Thermal resistance (R-value): Duct insulation is rated by R-value; ducts running through unconditioned attics or crawl spaces in a Victorville climate typically require R-6 or R-8 insulation to limit heat gain in summer.
  • Cross-sectional area: Cross-sectional area, measured in square inches, determines how much air a duct can carry at a given velocity; undersized cross-sections create high static pressure that strains the blower motor.
  • Length: Total duct length affects static pressure and airflow balance; longer runs require larger diameters or higher blower capacity to maintain adequate CFM at the register.

How HVAC ductwork relates to the broader system

HVAC ductwork is a part of the larger HVAC system and connects directly to the air conditioning unit, the furnace or air handler, and every supply and return register in the building. The duct network transports conditioned air as the final step in the refrigeration cycle: the unit conditions the air, and the ducts deliver it. A heat pump relies on the same duct network for both heating and cooling, which makes duct condition especially important in climates like Victorville's High Desert where the equipment switches modes seasonally.

Ductwork interacts with zone control systems, which use motorized dampers inside the ducts to direct airflow to specific areas of the home independently. Zone control does not eliminate the need for ducts; it adds a layer of control over the existing duct network. The true alternative to a ducted system is a ductless mini split system, which delivers conditioned air directly through wall-mounted air handlers and requires no duct network at all. Ductless systems are common in room additions or older homes where running new ductwork is not practical.

Common HVAC ductwork problems and signs of failure

HVAC ductwork problems fall into four main categories: leaks, blockages, poor insulation, and improper sizing. Each one reduces system efficiency and comfort in a measurable way.

  • Duct leaks: Duct leaks at joints, seams, or punctures allow conditioned air to escape into attics or crawl spaces before it reaches living areas, driving up energy use and reducing comfort.
  • Blockages: Blockages from debris, collapsed flexible duct, or closed dampers restrict airflow and create pressure imbalances that make some rooms too warm and others too cold.
  • Poor insulation: Ducts with degraded or missing insulation in unconditioned spaces absorb heat from the surrounding attic, which can raise supply-air temperature by 10 degrees or more before it reaches the register.
  • Undersized ducts: Undersized ducts create high static pressure that forces the blower to work harder, shortening equipment life and increasing noise at registers.

Signs that ductwork needs inspection or HVAC ductwork replacement include uneven room temperatures, higher-than-normal utility bills, excessive dust near registers, and audible whistling or rattling from inside the walls or ceiling. In the Victorville area, attic temperatures regularly exceed 140 degrees Fahrenheit in summer, which accelerates deterioration of flexible duct and duct tape used at connections. Where HVAC ductwork applies

HVAC ductwork applies to any building that uses a central forced-air system. This includes single-family homes, multi-family buildings, light commercial spaces, and new construction. Residential systems in the Victorville area most commonly use a combination of sheet-metal trunk lines and flexible branch ducts routed through attic spaces. Commercial buildings often use larger-diameter galvanized steel ducts with higher CFM ratings to serve open floor plans. Ductwork is not used in every system. Ductless mini split installations and radiant heating systems deliver heat through floors, walls, or ceilings rather than through air channels, making ductwork unnecessary in those applications.
