What is a failed compressor (compressor burnout)

A failed compressor, called compressor burnout, is a type of AC component failure in which the compressor can no longer pressurize refrigerant through the cooling cycle, causing the entire air conditioning system to stop producing cold air. The compressor is the mechanical heart of any central air conditioning system, and when it fails, no amount of refrigerant recharge or thermostat adjustment restores cooling. A broken compressor is not a minor repair. It is the single most consequential component failure in residential and light-commercial HVAC.

Failed compressor is a sub-category of AC component failure, sitting above refrigerant leaks and capacitor faults in severity because the compressor itself is the load-bearing unit of the refrigeration cycle. A functioning compressor compresses low-pressure refrigerant vapor into high-pressure, high-temperature gas; a non-operational compressor produces zero pressure differential, so the refrigerant never moves and the system never cools.

Measurable attributes of a failed compressor

A failed compressor diagnosis relies on specific, measurable deviations from normal operating parameters rather than guesswork. Six attributes confirm the fault with certainty.

  • Operating pressure loss: Operating pressure loss is the clearest single indicator. A healthy compressor maintains suction and discharge pressure within the system's rated range, while a failed unit reads 0 PSI output on the high side, meaning refrigerant is not being moved at all.
  • Electrical draw anomaly: Electrical draw anomaly shows up as an amperage deviation greater than 20% from the compressor's rated draw; a seized motor pulls far above rated amps before tripping the breaker, while burnt windings pull far below or nothing at all.
  • Voltage irregularity: Voltage at the compressor terminals should match supply voltage within a tight tolerance; a significant drop or spike at the compressor leads to motor winding failure over time and is both a cause and a measurable symptom of compressor burnout.
  • No cooling output: No cooling output means the supply air temperature stays above the thermostat setpoint regardless of run time. A direct consequence of zero refrigerant circulation when the compressor is non-operational.
  • Abnormal noise level: Abnormal noise level at startup. Grinding, clicking, or a hard mechanical bang. Points to a seized piston assembly or failed start capacitor forcing the motor to stall rather than spin up.
  • Refrigerant leakage: Refrigerant leakage, measured as a pressure drop in kPa or PSI over a timed hold test, sometimes accompanies compressor failure when internal valve damage allows refrigerant to migrate across the high and low sides without being pumped.
  • Temperature deviation: Temperature deviation at the condenser coil. Where discharge temperatures should reach 100°F to 130°F under normal load. Drops sharply when the compressor is not compressing, confirming the fault from the thermal side.

A licensed technician performing a failed compressor diagnosis checks all of these parameters together. A single reading in isolation can mislead; the full picture. Pressure, amperage, voltage, temperature and noise. Confirms whether the compressor is the root cause or a downstream victim of another fault.

Component parts involved in compressor failure

A failed compressor is not a single-part event. The compressor assembly contains several components, and failure in any one of them produces the same end result: a non-operational compressor that cannot sustain refrigerant pressure.

  • Seized piston assembly: Seized piston assembly stops mechanical compression entirely, usually from lubrication loss or liquid refrigerant slugging back into the cylinder.
  • Burnt motor windings: Burnt motor windings result from sustained voltage irregularity, overheating, or repeated hard starts, and they leave carbon contamination throughout the refrigerant circuit that must be flushed before any replacement compressor is installed.
  • Failed start capacitor: Failed start capacitor prevents the motor from reaching operating speed, causing it to draw locked-rotor amperage until the thermal overload trips. A pattern that damages windings over repeated cycles even before full compressor burnout occurs.
  • Internal valves: Internal valves. The reed or scroll valves that separate suction from discharge. Can crack or warp under thermal stress, allowing refrigerant to bypass rather than compress, which produces low pressure readings without a seized piston.
  • Pump mechanism: Pump mechanism wear in scroll compressors shows up as gradual pressure loss over months rather than sudden failure, making early diagnosis harder but no less necessary.

How a failed compressor relates to the broader AC system

A failed compressor causes complete air conditioning system shutdown. Not partial cooling, but zero cooling. Because every other component in the refrigeration loop depends on the compressor to move refrigerant. The evaporator coil cannot absorb heat without refrigerant flow. The condenser coil cannot reject heat. The expansion valve has nothing to meter. The result is a system that runs its fan, draws power and produces no temperature change at the supply registers.

The relationship extends to connected equipment. A heat pump uses the same compressor-driven refrigeration cycle in both heating and cooling modes, so a failed compressor in a heat pump eliminates both functions simultaneously. A ductless mini split system houses its compressor in the outdoor unit, and a compressor burnout there takes down every indoor air handler connected to that outdoor unit, not only one zone.

Compressor failure requires system-level repair. Burnt motor windings release acid and carbon into the refrigerant circuit, contaminating the metering device, evaporator coil, and lineset. Replacing the compressor alone is insufficient. Without flushing the circuit and replacing the filter drier, repeat failure is the predictable result.

Signs of a failed compressor: failed compressor AC not cooling

Failed compressor AC not cooling is the most common presenting symptom, but several other signs appear before or alongside total cooling loss. Recognizing them early reduces the risk of secondary contamination damage to the rest of the system.

  • No cold air at registers: No cold air at registers despite the outdoor unit running is the primary sign. The fan motor and controls operate normally, but supply air stays at or above room temperature.
  • Hard start or no start: Hard start or no start at the outdoor unit. A clicking sound followed by the unit shutting off within seconds. Indicates the compressor motor is stalling, often from a failed start capacitor or seized internals.
  • Tripped breaker: Tripped breaker on the compressor circuit, especially a breaker that resets but trips again within minutes, points to locked-rotor amperage from a seized piston assembly.
  • Warm outdoor unit: Warm outdoor unit with no vibration or compressor noise means the compressor is not running at all, even if the condenser fan is spinning.
  • Refrigerant pressure flat on both sides: Refrigerant pressure flat on both sides of the system. Suction and discharge reading the same. Confirms zero compression is occurring.
  • Burning smell: Burning smell near the outdoor unit or at the air handler indicates motor winding failure, a sign of compressor burnout rather than a minor electrical fault.

Diagnose failed compressor: what a proper evaluation covers

To diagnose a failed compressor accurately, a technician performs a structured sequence of electrical and mechanical checks before condemning the unit. Misdiagnosis is costly. Replacing a compressor when the actual fault is a failed capacitor or low voltage from the utility wastes the most expensive repair in residential HVAC.

The evaluation begins at the disconnect. Voltage is measured there and at the compressor terminals to confirm the unit is receiving correct supply voltage. Amperage draw is then measured on all three legs for three-phase units, or both legs for single-phase units, and compared against the nameplate rated load amps. Suction and discharge pressures are read with the system running, or attempted to run, to confirm whether any compression is occurring. A megohm test follows. It identifies insulation breakdown in the motor windings that indicates burnout. Finally, a capacitor test rules out the start capacitor as the sole cause before the compressor itself is condemned.

A non-operational compressor confirmed by this sequence requires replacement or full system evaluation for a new installation.
