An ignition system, called an ignition module or spark ignition system, is a heating system component. It generates a high-voltage spark or intense heat to ignite the fuel-air mixture inside a furnace or boiler, triggering combustion so the heat exchanger can warm the air distributed through the home. Ignition systems sit at the start of every heating cycle. Their function is critical: they determine whether the burner fires at all. Parts of an ignition system

An ignition system is made up of several components that work together to produce and confirm a flame. The igniter electrode is the tip that actually produces the spark, positioned across a precise gap from the burner. A high-voltage ignition control module sends the electrical pulse down high-voltage wiring to the electrode at the right moment in the heating cycle. A flame sensor then confirms that combustion has occurred, signaling the control module to hold the gas valve open. Without a working flame sensor, the control module shuts the gas valve within seconds as a safety measure, even when the igniter fires correctly.

Measurable attributes of an ignition system

Ignition systems operate within tight electrical and mechanical tolerances. The spark gap between the igniter electrode and the burner ground typically measures 0.020 to 0.060 inches; a gap outside that range produces a weak or absent spark. Voltage output at the electrode runs from 10 to 20 kV, supplied by the ignition control module through shielded high-voltage wiring. The entire ignition sequence timing from call-for-heat to confirmed flame spans only milliseconds, though the control module allows a trial-for-ignition window of a few seconds before locking out. Spark energy at the electrode is measured in millijoules (mJ), and operating voltage to the control module itself is standard line voltage, typically 24 V on the low-voltage control circuit. Temperature at the burner surface during ignition can reach several hundred degrees Celsius within the first combustion cycle. These measurements matter during igniter diagnostics because a reading outside any one of these ranges points directly to the failing component.

How the ignition system fits into the heating system

The ignition system is part of the broader heating system and works in sequence with the gas valve, the inducer motor and the heat exchanger. When the thermostat calls for heat, the control board energizes the inducer motor first to purge combustion gases, then signals the ignition control module to begin the spark sequence. Gas flows only after the module confirms the igniter is active, keeping unburned fuel from accumulating in the combustion chamber. Once the flame sensor verifies combustion, heat transfers through the heat exchanger to the air handler. A failure anywhere in this chain stops the heating cycle, which is why ignition system troubleshooting follows the sequence rather than jumping straight to part replacement.

Ignition system vs. Pilot light systems

A modern spark ignition system is the opposite of a standing pilot light or continuous pilot. Pilot lights burn constantly. A pilot light burns a small gas flame around the clock, using fuel continuously whether the furnace fires or not. A spark ignition system produces a spark only when the thermostat calls for heat, then shuts off completely between cycles. Intermittent ignition systems are standard on furnaces manufactured after the early 1990s because they eliminate the constant gas draw of a continuous pilot. A fuel shutoff valve is a related but distinct component that controls gas flow to the burner and is separate from the ignition module, though both must function correctly for the burner to fire safely.

Common signs of a faulty ignition system

A faulty ignition system produces recognizable symptoms before the furnace stops heating entirely. The most common sign is repeated clicking with no fire. This indicates the spark is present but combustion is not being established or confirmed. A furnace that locks out after one or two heating attempts and requires a manual reset points to the ignition control module or flame sensor rather than the gas supply. Visible cracks or carbon tracking on the igniter electrode can cause intermittent sparking, and hairline fractures may widen under thermal stress. Short ignition time windows, where the burner lights briefly then shuts down within seconds, usually mean the flame sensor is coated with oxidation and cannot read the flame signal reliably.
