### What a ductless mini-split air conditioner is

A ductless mini-split air conditioner is a split-system cooling unit whose indoor and outdoor components are connected by a refrigerant line rather than by supply and return ducts. The outdoor side houses the compressor and condenser coil; the indoor side is a wall-mounted or ceiling-cassette air handler with its own evaporator coil and fan. Because no ductwork is involved, the mini-split system avoids the energy losses that duct leakage causes in a [central air conditioning system](https://ac-plus-heating-air.mintlify.site/central-air-conditioning-system), which typically loses 20 to 30 percent of conditioned air through leaky ducts before it reaches the living space. Each indoor unit operates independently, so rooms that are unoccupied draw no cooling energy.

### How a ductless mini-split air conditioner relates to other cooling systems

A ductless mini-split air conditioner is a type of Air Conditioner, the same broad category that includes central ducted systems, window units and portable coolers. The clearest contrast is with a Central Ducted Air Conditioner: a central system moves conditioned air through a duct network that runs through walls, attics and crawl spaces, while a mini-split system delivers conditioned air directly from the wall-mounted air handler into the room. A Window Air Conditioner is self-contained in a single chassis and cools only the room it sits in, whereas a multi-zone mini-split AC can cool several rooms from one outdoor compressor. When the refrigerant cycle reverses to provide heat, the unit is correctly called a [heat pump](https://ac-plus-heating-air.mintlify.site/heat-pump), making it a year-round system rather than a cooling-only appliance. ### Common problems and signs a mini-split system needs service

A ductless mini-split air conditioner shows recognizable signs when it needs attention. Reduced airflow from the indoor air handler usually points to a clogged filter or a dirty evaporator coil, both of which restrict the air path and force the compressor to work harder. Ice on the indoor unit or refrigerant line set indicates low refrigerant charge or a blocked coil. A unit that runs continuously without reaching the set temperature may have a refrigerant leak, a failing compressor, or a line set that exceeds the rated length for the outdoor unit. Grinding or rattling from the outdoor compressor signals a mechanical problem inside the compressor housing. Water dripping from the indoor air handler indoors rather than through the condensate drain line means the drain is blocked. Refrigerant work requires EPA 608-certified handling. For that reason, a licensed technician should perform any repair that involves opening the refrigerant circuit.
